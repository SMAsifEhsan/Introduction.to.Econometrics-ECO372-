An estimator is a function of data applied to a random sample, so it has its own sampling distribution. Good estimators are unbiased and efficient in small samples, and consistent and asymptotically efficient as the sample grows. Pick a property, and switch the estimator between the sample mean X and the OLS slope β1 to see each idea twice.

How to read these diagrams

Each draw is a fresh random sample. We compute the estimator, drop it into a histogram, and repeat thousands of times — so the histogram is the sampling distribution. The solid vertical line is the true parameter; the dashed line is the average of our estimates. Unbiased means those two coincide; efficient means the histogram is narrow; consistent means it collapses onto the truth as n grows. The dotted bell is the matching theoretical normal.

R² says how much of the variation in test scores the regression accounts for. The total variation splits exactly into an explained part and a residual part: TSS = ESS + RSS, and R² = ESS / TSS. Change the scenario and watch the explained (green) share of the bar grow or shrink.

Common mistake

A high R² does not prove causality. It only says that the fitted line explains a larger share of the variation in the sample. Causal claims need a research design and assumptions, not only a fitted line.

So far the slope has been a single number: one extra unit of X moves Y by β1, no matter where you start. That fails in two ways — when the effect of X depends on the level of X, and when it depends on another regressor. The fix keeps OLS: the model stays linear in the parameters while becoming nonlinear in the variables.

Learning goalRead a slope that changes — and always report an effect together with the point where it was evaluated.
Try thisOn the quadratic, move income from 10 to 40 and watch one model deliver two very different slopes.
Why OLS still works here

A quadratic looks nonlinear, but writing X1 = Income and X2 = Income² turns it into an ordinary two-regressor multiple regression — linear in β₀, β₁, β₂, so OLS applies unchanged. This is not perfect multicollinearity either: perfect collinearity needs an exact linear relation between the regressors, and Income² is exact but nonlinear. Contrast Y = β₀X^β₁ + u, which is nonlinear in the parameters and cannot be estimated by OLS at all.

Panel data follows the same entities over time. That repetition is what lets us delete an omitted variable we cannot even measure: anything that differs across states but stays fixed over time — a culture of drinking and driving, say — can be differenced or demeaned away, without ever observing it.

Learning goalSee how within-entity variation identifies an effect that the cross-section gets backwards.
Try thisStart on the cross-section — the slope is positive. Then switch to fixed effects and watch the sign flip.
What fixed effects can and cannot remove

Entity fixed effects absorb anything constant within a state, however unobservable. Time fixed effects absorb anything common to all states in a given year — a national tariff, a federal campaign. Together they handle omitted variables that vary across entities or across time. What they cannot touch is an omitted variable that varies across both at once; that is the problem instrumental variables are for.

When Y is 0 or 1, the regression function becomes a probability: E(Y | X) = Pr(Y = 1 | X). A straight line will happily predict a probability of 1.3 or −0.2, so we run the index β₀ + β₁X through a cumulative distribution function instead — the normal CDF for probit, the logistic CDF for logit.

Learning goalSee why a probability model needs an S-curve, and why its marginal effect is not one number.
Try thisOn the LPM, push β₁ up until the fitted line leaves the 0–1 band at both ends.
Why not just use OLS?

Two reasons. The fitted line is unbounded, so predicted probabilities can fall outside [0, 1] — a meaningless answer. And the error is heteroskedastic by construction: for a 0/1 outcome Var(Y) = P(1 − P), which depends on X through P and so can never be constant. Probit and logit fix the first problem by construction; because they are nonlinear in the parameters, they are estimated by maximum likelihood rather than OLS.

When the regressor is endogenous — correlated with the error — OLS is biased and no amount of data repairs it. If the omitted factor cannot be measured and does not sit still over time, fixed effects cannot help either. Instrumental variables take the part of X that is driven by something unrelated to the error, and use only that part to explain Y.

Learning goalUnderstand the two conditions an instrument must satisfy, and the price paid for using one.
Try thisWeaken the instrument and watch the IV estimate stay centred but scatter wildly.
The two conditions, in plain words

Relevance: Corr(Z, X) ≠ 0 — the instrument actually moves the regressor, otherwise there is nothing to work with. Exogeneity: Corr(Z, u) = 0 — the instrument reaches Y only through X, with no direct path of its own. Relevance is testable from the first stage; exogeneity is an assumption you must defend with economics, because the error is never observed.
